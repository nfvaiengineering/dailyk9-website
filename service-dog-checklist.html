Owner-Trained Service Dog Intake Checklist

Work through this before your free intake call. Every item comes up on the call.

  1. Name the barriers a dog could help with.

    Panic, sensory overload, blood sugar or other medical changes, balance, getting out the door. Share only what you're comfortable with. No doctor's letter is required for public access under the ADA; housing and air travel have their own rules.

  2. List up to five tasks you need.

    For example: deep pressure, interrupting repetitive behaviors, alerting to a medical change, retrieving medication, bracing, guided exit. See types of service dogs.

  3. Write down where you most need access.

    Work, school, the grocery store, the airport, medical appointments.

  4. Profile your dog as a prospect.

    Breed, age, sex, spay or neuter status, history, food and toy drive (1 to 10), and sensitivity to sounds, surfaces, strangers, or other dogs. Be honest about reactivity.

  5. Rate your dog's obedience today.

    For heel, sit, down, stay, come, place, leave it, and look, score distance, duration, and distraction from 1 to 10, or X if untrained. Public access starts with control.

  6. Gather your vet records.

    Current rabies, DHPP, and Bordetella, a recent wellness exam, and parasite prevention. A clean bill of health is required before training begins, and brace or mobility tasks need vet clearance. You can upload records here.

  7. Count your weekly training time.

    1 to 3, 4 to 6, or 7+ hours a week between sessions. Owner-training runs 12 to 24 months, and you do the daily training with coaching.

  8. Plan the budget.

    Most owner-trained teams land roughly $4,000 to $9,000, billed monthly over 12 to 24 months, versus $20,000 to $40,000 for a program placement. No handler is turned away for ability to pay alone. Details on programs and pricing.

  9. Know the Arizona rules.

    A.R.S. § 11-1024(G) lets a trainer take a service animal in training into public places for training. A.R.S. § 11-1024(K) sets a civil penalty of up to $250 for misrepresenting an animal as a service animal. There is no official registry, so skip anyone selling registration, ID cards, or certificates.

  10. Ask about timing.

    Nicole works with a maximum of two active service dog teams at a time, so ask about the next opening when you call.

Checklist done? Book the call.

Bring your answers. Nicole will tell you honestly whether your dog is a prospect.
